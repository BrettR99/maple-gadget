Gaming headsets get expensive fast, but the truth is you don't need to spend $200+ to hear footsteps clearly and have your teammates hear you. Under $150 CAD there are genuinely good options — and a lot of junk. This roundup separates the two.

Every headset below is widely available in Canada from retailers like Best Buy Canada, Amazon.ca, and GameStop, and typically sells for under CA$150. Prices in this article are approximate Canadian prices observed in September 2026 — they move around with sales, so check the current price before you buy.

## How this roundup was built

This is a **research roundup, not a hands-on review**. Maple Gadget did not receive or test these headsets in person — our launch budget is $0, and we won't pretend otherwise. Instead, the rankings below are built from:

- **Measurement-based reviews** from outlets with real testing rigs: RTINGS, Tom's Hardware, Tom's Guide, PCWorld, PCGamesN, GamesRadar, Pocket-lint, and Dexerto
- **Long-term ownership reports** from established outlets like Hardware Canucks
- **Manufacturer spec sheets** (used for specifications only — never as evidence that a product performs well)
- **Canadian retail listings** at Best Buy Canada, Amazon.ca, and GameStop for pricing and availability

What we did *not* use: Amazon customer review text, user star ratings, content-farm "top 10" listicles, AI-generated review spam, or sponsored reviews without clear disclosure. Reddit threads were consulted only as background color for common comfort and durability complaints, never as the basis for a ranking.

The criteria, in order: **sound quality for gaming** (positional cues, clarity), **microphone quality**, **comfort over long sessions**, **build quality**, then **Canadian price and availability**. Wireless is a bonus, not a requirement — at this price, wired usually sounds better per dollar.

Scores are Maple Gadget's own judgment based on the evidence above. Nobody paid for placement, and nobody can — we have no affiliate relationships, and rankings are never for sale.

## The picks

### 1. SteelSeries Arctis Nova 3 — Best overall

**Score: 8.7/10 · Around $120–130 CAD**

The Nova 3 is the safest recommendation under $150. It connects over USB-C (plus 3.5mm for everything else), works with SteelSeries' GG and Sonar software on PC for EQ and spatial audio, and has a retractable ClearCast Gen 2 microphone that tucks into the earcup when you're done.

Why it wins: reviewers keep landing in the same place. Tom's Guide concluded it's hard to find major faults at this price — it fits well, sounds great, and is easy to customize. PCWorld praised its warm, punchy audio and lightweight build with the retractable mic as a standout. RTINGS has it in their measured comparison database alongside the rest of the Nova line, and it holds up.

**Pros:** Excellent sound for the money; comfortable for long sessions; retractable mic; USB-C + 3.5mm covers PC, PlayStation, Switch, and mobile.

**Cons:** Wired only; lets in a fair bit of ambient noise; runs small, so large heads should try before buying; Xbox gets stereo only via 3.5mm.

**Best for:** PC and PlayStation gamers who want the best sound under $150 and don't mind a cable.

### 2. HyperX Cloud II — Best microphone and build

**Score: 8.3/10 · Around $80–120 CAD**

The Cloud II is old — it launched in 2015 — and it still shows up in "best budget headset" conversations for a reason. The aluminum frame survives years of daily use, the detachable microphone is genuinely good, and the included USB sound card adds virtual 7.1 on PC. RTINGS' measurements put it ahead of competitors like the Corsair Void Pro on microphone quality, and GamingTrend scored it 95/100 in their review.

The trade-offs are age-related: no Bluetooth, no modern app ecosystem, and limited EQ compared to newer headsets. But nothing at this price feels this solid, and replacement earpads and cables are easy to find — which matters for something you'll wear daily for years.

**Pros:** Tank-like aluminum build; detachable mic with strong measured performance; USB sound card included; proven long-term durability.

**Cons:** Dated design; no wireless; software/EQ options are limited; prices vary a lot between retailers.

**Best for:** PC gamers who want a headset that lasts and a mic their teammates will actually compliment.

### 3. Corsair HS65 Surround — Best sound tuning

**Score: 8.1/10 · Around $90–110 CAD**

The HS65 Surround is the tweaker's pick. It ships with 50mm drivers, a 3.5mm connection plus a USB adapter, Dolby 7.1 on PC and Mac, and Corsair's SoundID feature, which builds a personalized EQ profile from a quick hearing test. Tom's Guide found the sound excellent on PC and the microphone clear, calling it the right product at the right price for the middle ground. PCGamesN called it "a fantastic gaming headset under $100," and GamesRadar praised its flat, detailed default tuning.

The caveats: Tom's Guide also noted compatibility is limited and console performance is inconsistent — this is a PC-first headset. The fit isn't for everyone, and the earpads don't seal perfectly.

**Pros:** Great detailed sound on PC; SoundID personalization actually works; comfortable memory-foam pads; flip-to-mute mic; 2-year warranty.

**Cons:** PC-first — consoles get a lesser experience; fit can be hit-or-miss; wired only.

**Best for:** PC gamers who like to tune their audio and want surround features without paying extra.

### 4. Razer BlackShark V2 X — Best for competitive FPS on a budget

**Score: 7.8/10 · Around $55–70 CAD**

The BlackShark V2 X strips Razer's pricier BlackShark V2 down to the essentials: 50mm TriForce drivers, a cardioid HyperClear mic, and a plain 3.5mm connection that works on PC, PlayStation, Xbox, and Switch. No detachable mic, no USB dongle — which is exactly why it's cheap.

A note on evidence: dedicated lab measurements of the V2 X specifically are thinner than for the other picks here. What exists is solid, though — Hardware Canucks, the Canadian outlet, liked it after roughly six months of near-daily use, and SoundGuys' testing notes confirm the core trade-offs (faux-leather pads instead of the V2's fabric, fixed mic). At this price, it's the cheapest way into a genuinely competent esports-style headset from a major brand.

**Pros:** Light (~240g); clear directional audio for shooters; works on every platform via 3.5mm; very cheap for what you get.

**Cons:** 7.1 surround is Windows-only software; mic is fixed, not detachable; thinner measurement coverage than rivals; faux-leather pads get warm.

**Best for:** Competitive FPS players on a tight budget who want a no-frills wired headset that works everywhere.

### 5. SteelSeries Arctis Nova 1 — Best lightweight comfort

**Score: 7.6/10 · Around $70–80 CAD**

At 236g with SteelSeries' AirWeave earcups and a stretchy suspension headband, the Nova 1 is one of the most comfortable cheap headsets you can buy — Tom's Hardware called it "a great buy at $60" (US), praising the audio quality for the price and the slim, lightweight design. The fully retractable mic is neat, and the 3.5mm connection works across PC, PlayStation, Xbox, and Switch.

The honest counterpoint comes from Tom's Guide, which found it merely good rather than impressive — and noted the HyperX Cloud Stinger 2 below is a better device for less money. The Nova 1's case is comfort and multi-platform simplicity, not value-per-dollar supremacy. It's also analog-only, so no software features.

**Pros:** Extremely light and comfortable; retractable mic; works on every platform; clean, understated look.

**Cons:** Analog-only, no software features; unremarkable value next to cheaper rivals; generic styling.

**Best for:** Gamers who prioritize comfort and want one headset that plugs into everything.

### 6. Logitech G435 Lightspeed — Best wireless on a budget

**Score: 7.4/10 · Around $60–70 CAD**

The G435 is the only wireless pick on this list, and it earns the spot: 165g, Lightspeed USB wireless plus Bluetooth, 40mm drivers, and an advertised 18-hour battery. Pocket-lint gave it 4/5, calling it comfortable and good-sounding for PC and PlayStation gamers.

But go in with eyes open. The beamforming mics (no boom arm) pick up background noise — Tech4Gamers, which tested it for two weeks, scored it 6.8/10 and flagged flimsy build quality and mic performance. There's no 3.5mm jack, no Xbox support, and Logitech openly sizes it for smaller heads and younger players — adults with large heads may find the fit tight. This is the pick for convenience, not for critical listening or serious voice chat.

**Pros:** Only wireless option under $70; extremely light; Lightspeed + Bluetooth covers PC, PlayStation, Switch, and phones; long battery life.

**Cons:** Mic quality is mediocre and picks up room noise; flimsy build; no 3.5mm; no Xbox; fit runs small.

**Best for:** Kids, casual gamers, and anyone who absolutely needs wireless under $70.

### 7. HyperX Cloud Stinger 2 — Cheapest worth buying

**Score: 7.2/10 · Around $40–50 CAD**

This is the floor — the cheapest headset we'd actually recommend. Tom's Guide's verdict was blunt: the Cloud Stinger 2 is a better device than the SteelSeries Arctis Nova 1, and cheaper. You get 50mm drivers, DTS Headphone:X spatial audio, a swivel-to-mute mic, memory foam and leatherette pads, and a 2-year warranty. Best Buy Canada lists it around $40–45 CAD, which is remarkable for a headset from a major brand that isn't embarrassing.

It's plastic, the sound is unrefined next to the picks above, and there's nothing premium about it. But as a first headset for a kid, a backup, or a "I just need something that works" purchase, nothing cheaper is worth your money.

**Pros:** Absurdly cheap for a name-brand headset; 50mm drivers; swivel-to-mute mic; 2-year warranty.

**Cons:** All plastic; sound is fine, not good; no software features; wired only.

**Best for:** The tightest budgets, kids' first headsets, and backups.

## What to look for (and what to ignore)

**Driver size isn't everything.** 50mm vs 40mm tells you almost nothing about sound quality — tuning matters more. Every headset on this list has been vetted by real reviewers; don't pick based on the spec sheet.

**Microphone type matters more than you think.** If you play multiplayer, a boom mic (Nova 3, Cloud II, HS65, BlackShark V2 X, Stinger 2) will sound dramatically better to your teammates than built-in beamforming mics (G435). Reviewers consistently flag this.

**Wireless under $70 means compromises.** The G435 is genuinely convenient, but you're trading mic quality and build for the lack of a cable. If you mostly play at a desk, wired gets you more for your money.

**Platform compatibility is the fine print.** USB features (7.1 surround, EQ software) are usually PC-only. Xbox is the pickiest console — check whether a headset's features actually work on your platform before buying. When in doubt, a plain 3.5mm connection works everywhere but does the least.

**Ignore "7.1 surround" as a buying criterion.** It's software processing, available free in Windows Sonic, Dolby Atmos for Headphones, or Tempest 3D Audio on PS5. Don't pay extra for the logo on the box.

**Comfort is personal.** Weight, clamping force, and earcup size vary — and budget headsets fit small heads better on average. If you're between picks, lighter usually wins for long sessions.

## Final verdict

If you buy one headset from this list, make it the **SteelSeries Arctis Nova 3**. It's the most complete package under $150 CAD: great sound, a good retractable mic, broad compatibility, and comfort that reviewers keep praising. Nothing else at this price does everything this well.

On a tighter budget, the **HyperX Cloud II** (around $80–120) is the long-haul pick with the best mic, and the **Razer BlackShark V2 X** (around $55–70) is the cheapest way to get competent competitive audio. If you need wireless, the **Logitech G435** is your only real option under $70 — just know what you're giving up. And if $50 is the ceiling, the **HyperX Cloud Stinger 2** is the cheapest headset worth owning.
